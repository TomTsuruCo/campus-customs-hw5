HW 5 Reflection

Evidence on this page: Ticket 101 · Ticket 102 · Ticket 103 · Cash

How I would grade the agents and why

Overall, I think the agents did a good job finding the information needed for the three tickets and following the limits set for them. However, getting the information right did not always mean that the issue was fully handled. In some cases, I still had to make the decision or finish the response myself. The shop started with $3,400 and ended with $160 after paying the rent and the vendor invoice.

To evaluate the agents, I looked at whether their information was accurate, whether the Boss chose the relevant agents, whether they stayed within their permissions, and whether their work gave me something I could act on. I also considered how many tokens, tool calls, and seconds each ticket required.

For ticket 101, the Bulldog tee, I would give the team a B+. Inventory correctly found that there were no size S tees available and that Bulldog Print Co was blocked because of an unpaid invoice. Accounting identified invoice 501 for $840, due on August 28, 2026, which was three days overdue. It also checked that the $3,400 balance was enough to cover the invoice, leaving $2,560 if that payment was considered by itself. The Boss then created the payment request for the correct amount.

The Boss involved Accounting, Customer Service, and Inventory, which made sense. There was no reason to involve Facilities. However, Customer Service did not save a draft, so the customer still needed a response. The run also used 27,377 tokens, took 21 seconds, and made 13 tool calls. Around 65% of those tokens were used by the Boss, and the ticket details were read four separate times. For a relatively simple question, this seems like a lot of repeated work.

For ticket 102, the rent payment, I would give the team an A. The Boss involved Accounting and Facilities, and the run ended with a request for me to approve the $2,400 payment. Accounting calculated that paying the rent from the initial $3,400 would leave $1,000, but it did not treat having enough money as permission to pay. I think this was an important part of the design because I still had control over the actual payment.

This was the fastest and least expensive run in terms of tokens. It took 10 seconds, used 19,469 tokens, and made 10 tool calls. Still, there was unnecessary repetition. The ticket details and financial information were each read three times, even though the information was the same.

For ticket 103, the hoodie discount request, I would give the team a B. The customer wanted 20 hoodies, but there were only 8 available, meaning the shop was short by 12. Accounting found that the list price was $58 and the unit cost was $22. There was no discount policy in the database, so the agents correctly avoided making up a discount or assuming they could approve one.

The problem was that the team could not take the request much further. The Boss said that the approval process for a price override was unclear. It also tried to change the ticket status to “awaiting human approval,” but the tool rejected this because that status required an actual payment request. The Boss reported the failure correctly, but I still had to write the resolution myself. The saved customer draft said the request was being checked, which no longer reflected the situation after I decided to decline it. This run used 24,455 tokens, took 18 seconds, and made 11 tool calls.

Looking at the agents individually, Accounting was the most consistent. It got the figures right across all three tickets and never made a payment itself. Inventory also performed well, although its performance depended on how specific its instructions were. In the first dry run, it skipped the vendor check for ticket 101. Adding a sentence to its prompt fixed that.

Facilities provided the correct information, but much of its work overlapped with Accounting. Customer Service was less consistent because it saved a draft for ticket 103 but not for ticket 101. This matters because its work would eventually be seen by the customer. The Boss generally coordinated the relevant agents correctly, but it used around 67% of the total 71,301 tokens across the three runs.

What happened compared with what I expected

I correctly predicted which agents would be involved in each ticket, but I did not always predict the order in which they would work. At first, I thought the order was a minor detail. After seeing the results, I think it can affect whether an agent has enough information to complete its task.

For ticket 101, I expected Inventory to go first, followed by Accounting and Customer Service. The actual order was Boss, Accounting, Customer Service, and then Inventory. This meant Customer Service worked before Inventory had reported its findings. It did not save a draft and said it could not make promises to the customer. I cannot say for sure that the order caused this, but it seems more useful to have Customer Service write after the other agents have collected the relevant information.

For ticket 102, I expected Facilities to go before Accounting, but the actual order was Boss, Accounting, and Facilities. The correct agents were involved, and the Boss created the expected payment request. Facilities also checked the financial information, which I had not expected and which repeated some of Accounting’s work.

Ticket 103 was closest to my prediction. The order was Boss, Inventory, Accounting, and Customer Service. Facilities was not involved, and no payment was requested. What I did not predict was the failed attempt to change the status to “awaiting human approval.” The ticket remained open until I closed it manually.

There were also a few things I had not considered in my original plan. In the first dry run of the rent ticket, the Boss said that a human decision was needed but did not actually create a payment request. This meant there was nothing for me to approve until I explicitly instructed it to create one. So telling the human that approval is needed is not necessarily enough. The agent also needs to take the step that makes approval possible.

Another thing I realized was that the tickets could not really be considered separately. The rent payment reduced the balance from $3,400 to $1,000, and the $840 invoice payment reduced it further to $160. Ticket 103 involved no payment. Buying the 12 missing hoodies would cost 12 × $22 = $264, which was $104 more than the remaining balance. I had planned the tickets individually, but they all depended on the same limited amount of money.

What would have been simpler with one agent and tools

I think most of the information gathering could have been done more simply by one agent with access to the relevant tools. Across the three tickets, the team used 71,301 tokens and made 34 tool calls. A lot of this involved different agents reading the same information.

For example, ticket 102 mainly required reading the ticket, which included the lease information, and checking the shop’s finances. Instead, the ticket details and financial snapshot were each read three times. Similarly, on ticket 101, four agents opened the same ticket. Inventory, Accounting, and Facilities had different instructions, but much of their work involved using the same tools to read information.

There were also technical problems with getting the agents to work together. The first real run failed because of a 2,500-token limit. Another problem occurred when the Boss could not change the ticket status because a required argument was missing. From this experience, a lot of the difficulty came from connecting the agents and tools properly.

I would probably combine Inventory, Accounting, and Facilities into one agent that gathers the information. I would keep Customer Service separate to prepare customer drafts once the facts are available. I would also keep clear restrictions on which agent can change ticket statuses.

Most importantly, I would keep human approval for payments. The $2,400 rent and $840 invoice were paid because a person approved them. I think this is particularly important when the business has so little cash available.

For these three tickets, a simpler setup would probably be enough: one main agent to gather information and coordinate the work, a separate agent to write customer responses, and a human to approve payments. The five-agent setup helped me understand the different roles, but I am not convinced that all five were necessary for these tasks.

Three additional problems the team could already handle

One example would be a customer asking for a crest mug. The database shows that CC-MUG-CREST has no stock available. Elm City Gifts supplies mugs and small goods, has a three-day lead time, and has no open invoice blocking it. Inventory could identify the stock shortage and check the vendor, and Customer Service could draft a response explaining the expected lead time without guaranteeing it. A human would still need to place the order. This would be similar to ticket 101, but without the unpaid invoice preventing progress.

A second example would be a customer asking for 10 blue hats. The shop has 40 hats available, with a unit cost of $6 and a list price of $22. Inventory could confirm that there is enough stock, and Accounting could calculate the $16 difference between the price and unit cost, or $160 for 10 hats before other expenses. Customer Service could then prepare a draft confirming availability and the normal price. Since no discount or restocking would be needed, this should be more straightforward than ticket 103.

A third example would be the next rent payment. After the previous payment, the next due date for lease 1 became October 2, 2026, and the rent remained $2,400. If the cash balance were still $160, the payment request would be refused because there would not be enough money. The team could identify and report the problem, although it could not solve the cash shortage itself.

Three problems the team could not solve and what I would add

The first problem is actually restocking products. For ticket 101, the shop needed a size S tee, and for ticket 103, it needed another 12 hoodies. The existing tools could identify the shortages and check vendors, but they could not create a purchase order or record newly received stock. Even after paying the $840 invoice to unblock the tee vendor, someone still had to place the order manually. The tee itself cost $8 and had a list price of $28.

I would add tools to create purchase orders and record stock when it arrives. A Purchasing agent could check the vendor’s products, lead time, and outstanding invoices, then prepare an order for human approval. I would also add a check on how the purchase would affect the money needed for upcoming rent. The system should show clearly when an order is unaffordable, such as the $264 hoodie order with only $160 remaining.

The second problem is tracking incoming cash and planning for future expenses. The current setup records money going out, but it does not include sales coming in. With only $160 left, the shop is $2,240 short of the next $2,400 rent payment. Even selling all 8 available hoodies at $58 would bring in only $464. This would increase the balance to $624, still leaving a shortage of $1,776.

I would add a sales or orders table, a tool to record sales, and a tool to forecast the cash balance over a chosen period. A Controller agent could review expected income and expenses before the Boss prepares payment requests. This would help the human see the effect of approving a payment on the next few weeks of operations.

The third problem is handling decisions that require a policy or communication with someone outside the shop. Ticket 103 showed this clearly. The agents knew the stock, price, and cost, but they had no policy telling them whether an order of 20 hoodies qualified for a discount. They also could not send the draft to the customer.

I would add a pricing policy with clear discount rules and a tool that allows the agents to check them. A Pricing agent could apply discounts within those rules and ask for approval for exceptions. I would also add an email tool that requires a human to approve sending, along with an inbox tool that can turn incoming messages into tickets.

The same communication tools could help with the landlord and vendors. For example, an agent could draft a request for a payment extension or payment plan, which a human would review before sending. This would give the team a way to help address the cash shortage beyond simply reporting that there is not enough money.

My main takeaway is that the agents were useful for gathering accurate information and preparing actions, but the overall process still needed human judgment. The shop could pay the rent and the outstanding invoice, but doing both left too little money to restock the missing hoodies. I would want the next version to make that connection clearer before asking me to approve payments. I would also simplify the repeated information gathering and give the system better tools for purchasing, sales, and cash planning.
